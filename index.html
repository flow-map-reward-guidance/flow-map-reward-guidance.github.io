TL;DR

Guidance is usually framed as sampling a reward-tilted distribution $\tilde\rho(x)\propto e^{r(x)}\rho(x)$, which requires stochastic, multi-particle dynamics that are expensive even for simple rewards. We instead pose guidance as a deterministic optimal control problem, yielding a hierarchy of algorithms in which the flow map emerges naturally and popular denoiser-based methods like DPS are recovered as the coarsest approximation. The result is Flow Map Reward Guidance (FMRG): highly efficient, training-free alignment that uses the flow map to both integrate and guide the flow along a single trajectory — matching or beating baselines with as few as 3 NFEs, up to a 70× speedup.

01 — Motivation

Samplers got fast. Guidance didn't.

In generative modeling, we rarely want just any sample — we want to tailor it to a user-specified reward $r$ that captures what we actually care about.

That reward might score, for example, aesthetic quality, agreement with a measurement, physical plausibility, or alignment with human intent. Guidance steers a pre-trained model toward high reward at inference time, with no extra training.

The standard framing — reward tilting
$\tilde\rho(x) \;\propto\; e^{r(x)}\,\rho(x)$
easy to write down — but a hard problem to solve
Problem 01
Hard to solve
Sampling $\tilde\rho$ is computationally expensive even for simple rewards. Multi-particle schemes like SMC need a high number of particles and steps; single-particle shortcuts like DPS rely on heuristic approximations, leaving their output poorly characterized.
Problem 02
A poor fit for modern samplers
Reward tilting is rooted in stochastic processes. But modern flow models — FLUX, Stable Diffusion 3 — are deterministic, and flow maps push them to just a few steps. Porting tilt-based alignment here means simulating stochasticity inside an ODE sampler.
The central question

Is there a principled framework for guiding flow-based generative models with very few function evaluations?

03 — Key idea

So do we need the reward tilt?

Not necessarily. Rather than sample the reward tilt, we pose guidance directly as deterministic optimal control: steer a single trajectory to maximize reward while staying close to the base flow.

Standard framing
$\tilde\rho(x)\propto e^{r(x)}\rho(x)$
sample the reward tilt — stochastic, many particles
→reframe
Our perspective
$\displaystyle\min_u \int_0^1 \tfrac{\lVert u_t\rVert^2}{2\lambda}\,dt - r(x_1^u)$
steer one trajectory, $\dot x_t^u = b_t(x_t^u)+u_t$
Exact optimal control
$u_t^* = \lambda\,\nabla X_{t,1}^{u^*}(x_t^*)^\top \nabla r\!\big(X_{t,1}^{u^*}(x_t^*)\big)$

The optimal control is characterized in closed form, and the flow map appears explicitly — the same flow map that integrates the flow also guides it.

A hierarchy of approximations

The exact optimum is circular — computing it needs the optimally-controlled flow map it is trying to produce. FMRG breaks the loop by swapping in the uncontrolled flow map — the solution in the small-$\lambda$ limit, equivalently the greedy optimal control. DPS, in turn, arises from approximating this solution with the denoiser estimate.

Exact-optimalintractable
$u_t^* = \lambda\,\nabla X_{t,1}^{u^*}(x_t^*)^\top \nabla r\!\big(X_{t,1}^{u^*}(x_t^*)\big)$
↓uncontrolled flow map  (small-$\lambda$ / greedy)
FMRGours
$u_t(x) = \lambda\,\nabla X_{t,1}(x)^\top \nabla r\!\big(X_{t,1}(x)\big)$
↓substitute one-step denoiser estimate
DPSprior work
$u_t^{\mathrm{DPS}}(x) = \lambda\,\nabla_x r\!\big(\hat x_1(x)\big),\;\;\hat x_1 = \mathbb{E}[x_1\mid x_t]$

DPS and many of its related derivatives (e.g., MPGD, FlowDPS, FlowChef) are best understood as coarse approximations of optimal control — not of the reward tilt.

04 — Method

Flow Map Reward Guidance

FMRG integrates the greedy controlled dynamics with operator splitting: each step alternates an exact flow-map step with a guidance step.

FMRG — one trajectory, vs. multi-particle reward tilt
FMRG alternates exact flow-map steps with reward-gradient steps along a single trajectory (top), in contrast to reward-tilt sampling (e.g. SMC), which needs many particles, resampling, and many steps (bottom).
Operator splitting — one step of FMRG
$\tilde x_{t_{k+1}} = X_{t_k,\,t_{k+1}}(x_{t_k})$flow-map step
$x_{t_{k+1}} = \tilde x_{t_{k+1}} + \Delta t_k\,\lambda\, u(\tilde x_{t_{k+1}})$guidance step
the flow map integrates the base dynamics exactly; the guidance step then steers toward reward
We arrive at two design decisions
Decision 1 — project the reward gradient?

Keeping the flow-map Jacobian (FMRG-J): we prove that it projects the reward gradient onto the data manifold, so a guided step stays on-manifold by construction.

Dropping the Jacobian (FMRG-E) saves memory and optimizes the reward directly, but the trajectory can drift off-manifold.

Decision 2 — how much to guide?
Reward $r(x)$ Base $\rho_1$ Reward tilt Greedy Greedy + early stopping

Greedy guidance can over-optimize the reward, collapsing sample diversity.

Early stopping — guiding only on an initial window, then running the uncontrolled flow — provably keeps it in check.

05 — Results

Matches or surpasses baselines with as few as 3 NFEs

A single FLUX.1-dev flow map, evaluated across four reward families — from simple $\ell_2$ reconstruction to a 7B vision-language model.

Inverse problems
FMRG outperforms DPS, FlowChef, and FlowDPS on super-resolution, deblurring, and inpainting — across both AFHQ and FFHQ. FMRG-E matches baselines that use 2–10× more NFEs.
Reward-guided generation
On GenEval, FMRG-J scores 0.80 — the best of any method — and matches FMTT's quality at NFE 20, a 70× compute reduction.
Style transfer
FMRG-J transfers the reference style while preserving content; DPS misses the style and FlowChef shows artifacts — exactly as the approximation hierarchy predicts.
VLM rewards
With a 7B vision-language reward, FMRG follows complex compositional prompts — object attributes, spatial relations, text — that unguided FLUX consistently misses.
